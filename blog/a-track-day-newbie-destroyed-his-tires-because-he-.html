A Track Day Newbie Destroyed His Tires Because He Ignored Speed Ratings

📅 2024-06-10 👤 Ryan Miller ⏱️ 7 min read 🏷️ Story
I got a text from a guy named Kevin on a Sunday evening. He'd just gotten back from his first track day at Grattan Raceway, about an hour west of Grand Rapids. His tires were destroyed. Not worn – destroyed. The outer edges of both front tires looked like someone had taken a cheese grater to them. Chunks of rubber missing. Steel cords showing in one spot. He was lucky he hadn't had a blowout at 110 miles per hour. "Ryan, what did I do wrong?" he asked. Kevin had a 2019 BMW M2 Competition. It came from the factory with Michelin Pilot Sport 4S tires, which have a Y speed rating – that means they're designed for sustained speeds over 186 mph. But Kevin's car had about 25,000 miles on it when he bought it used. The previous owner had replaced the original Michelins with a set of all-season tires. All-seasons. On an M2. On a race track. I asked him what tires he was running. He sent me a photo of the sidewall: "245/35R19 91V." The V speed rating is good for 149 mph. That's fine for highway driving. The Michigan interstate speed limit is 70. But on a race track, even a novice driver can hit 120-130 on the straights. The problem isn't the peak speed – it's the heat. A V-rated tire at 120 mph generates far more heat than a Y-rated tire at the same speed. The tread compound gets too soft. The rubber tears. The edges chunk off. That's exactly what happened to Kevin. The Tire Speed Rating Guide would have shown him the difference before he ever bought a track day ticket.

I explained to Kevin that speed ratings aren't just about top speed. They're about heat tolerance. A tire rated for 186 mph can dissipate heat much better than a tire rated for 149 mph. On a track, where you're braking hard, cornering hard, and accelerating hard repeatedly, the heat builds up fast. The all-season tires on his M2 were designed for cold mornings and grocery runs. They weren't designed for sustained lateral loads at triple-digit speeds. Kevin asked if he could have just inflated the tires more to reduce heat. I told him no. Higher pressure reduces contact patch, which reduces grip, which makes the car handle worse. And it doesn't solve the fundamental problem: the rubber compound isn't designed for track temperatures. He asked if he could have taken it easier on the corners. I told him that defeats the purpose of a track day. He asked if the tires would have survived if he'd only done three laps instead of six. I told him maybe. But he did six laps. Then he did another session. Then another. By the end of the day, the tires were junk. Kevin's story is frustrating because it was avoidable. He didn't know that speed ratings matter for track use. He didn't know that the previous owner had downgraded the tires. He didn't even know what speed rating his car originally came with. I asked him to check the driver's door jamb for the factory tire size and load rating. It showed "245/35R19 93Y" – the Y rating is higher than the V rating he had. The previous owner had put on tires with a lower load index too: 91 instead of 93. That's a difference of about 100 pounds per tire of load capacity. The Tire Load Index Calculator would have caught that too.

Kevin's M2 weighs about 3,600 pounds. With driver and a half tank of gas, call it 3,800. That's 950 pounds per corner. A 91 load index tire is rated for about 1,350 pounds. So he had margin – plenty of it. But the speed rating was the real issue. I've seen this pattern before. People buy used performance cars. The previous owner put cheaper tires on before selling it. The new owner assumes the tires are fine because they have tread left. Then they take the car to a track day or an autocross, and the tires fail spectacularly. Sometimes it's not just chunking. Sometimes it's a blowout. I remember a guy named Steve who brought his Mustang GT to Waterford Hills. He was running all-season tires that were three years old. The front left tire let go in Turn 5. He spun into the grass, hit a tire barrier, and caused $6,000 in damage to his car. He was fine. His car wasn't. The tire had overheated, the tread had separated, and the sidewall had blown out. He told me "I didn't know tires could get too hot." They can. Kevin had to buy a new set of tires. He ordered Michelin Pilot Sport 4S with the correct Y speed rating and 93 load index. The set cost him $1,400 mounted and balanced. He also bought a spare set of wheels with track-only tires – Nitto NT01s – for future track days. He learned his lesson. The hard way. Now, here's the thing about speed ratings that most people don't know. You can go up in speed rating without any downside. Going down is dangerous. If your car came with Y-rated tires, you can put on W-rated tires (168 mph) or Y-rated or even Z-rated. That's fine. But putting on V-rated or H-rated tires on a performance car is asking for trouble, especially if you ever drive hard. The Tire Speed Rating Guide has a chart that shows you the temperature resistance of each rating. Higher ratings use different rubber compounds and construction methods that handle heat better. That's not marketing. That's engineering. Kevin asked me if he'd have the same problem if he only drove his M2 on the street. I told him no. On the street, even spirited driving, you're not generating sustained heat the way you do on a track. The all-season tires would have been fine for daily driving. But he bought an M2 to drive it hard. And he drove it hard. The tires couldn't keep up. He's not alone. Every year, guys show up to track days with mismatched tires, old tires, underinflated tires, overinflated tires, tires with the wrong speed rating, tires with dry rot. The organizers do a basic tech inspection – tread depth, pressure, visible damage – but they don't check speed ratings. That's on the driver. Kevin could have compared the factory tire size (245/35R19 93Y) to the tires on the car (245/35R19 91V) and seen that while the size was the same, the load index and speed rating were wrong. He didn't know to look. Now he does. Speed ratings matter. Load ratings matter. Tires are not just black circles. They are engineered components that have to match the car's performance envelope. If you drive a performance car, buy performance tires. If you drive a truck, buy truck tires. If you drive a car that tows, buy tires rated for towing. Don't trust the previous owner. Don't trust the tire shop. Check the sidewall yourself. Kevin still has his M2. He's planning another track day this fall. He's buying a set of track wheels and tires – dedicated ones. He's going to use the speed rating guide to pick the right rubber. "I'm not making that mistake again," he said.

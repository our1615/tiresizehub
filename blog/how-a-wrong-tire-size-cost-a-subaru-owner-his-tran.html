How a Wrong Tire Size Cost a Subaru Owner His Transmission (And $4,000)

📅 2024-06-10 👤 Ryan Miller ⏱️ 7 min read 🏷️ Story
The tow truck driver called it in around 6 PM on a Thursday. “Gary’s Transmission, we got a silver Outback, blew the center diff somewhere around mile marker 82 on I-94.” I was sitting in my home office in Detroit, scrolling through emails, when my phone buzzed. The shop foreman, a guy named Pete who’d been rebuilding transmissions since before I was born, sent me a photo. The center differential looked like someone had filled it with metal shavings and angry glitter. “Subaru?” I typed back. “Yeah. Customer says he put bigger wheels on it last month. Didn’t check the size.” I knew the rest before Pete even explained. All-wheel drive systems hate mismatched tires. Hate them. The Subaru’s center differential is a mechanical unit that expects all four wheels to spin at almost exactly the same speed. When you put tires that are different diameters – even a quarter-inch difference in radius – the differential is constantly slipping to compensate. It overheats. The fluid breaks down. Then the gears eat themselves. The customer’s name was Chris. He was twenty-nine, worked as a delivery driver for a pharmacy chain in Ann Arbor. His Outback was a 2016 model he’d bought used two years earlier. He loved that car. He washed it every Sunday, waxed it twice a year, and had a little trash can in the back seat so his passengers wouldn’t leave wrappers on the floor. When he decided to upgrade the wheels, he did what a lot of people do – he found a set of used wheels on Facebook Marketplace that looked cool. The seller said they were from a newer Outback. “They’ll fit,” the seller wrote. “Both are 5x114.3.” Chris didn’t ask about tire size. He didn’t ask about offset. He didn’t ask about the load rating or the speed rating or whether the tires were all the same circumference. He handed over four hundred dollars and drove home with five wheels – four with tires mounted, one spare without. The tires on those wheels were 225/55R18. His stock tires were 225/60R17. He didn’t know what those numbers meant. He just knew the wheels looked good. For two weeks, the Outback drove fine. No warning lights. No weird noises. The tires hummed a little on the highway, but he figured that was normal for a bigger wheel. Then he took a trip from Detroit to Chicago to visit his brother. Somewhere near Michigan City, Indiana, the AWD warning light started flashing. Then the transmission started whining. Then, about fifteen minutes later, the car lost power. He coasted to the shoulder. Called AAA. The tow truck came. The driver asked where he wanted to go. Chris said “the nearest Subaru dealer.” The driver said “there’s a transmission shop in Gary that knows Subarus.” Chris agreed. That’s how he ended up in Pete’s bay at Gary’s Transmission. Pete called me because he knew I’d been writing about this stuff. “Another one,” he said. “You want to talk to the kid?” I did. Chris got on the phone. He sounded tired. Not angry yet – just confused. “Ryan, I don’t understand,” he said. “How can tires break a transmission?” I asked him what size tires he put on. He read me the sidewall: 225/55R18. I asked him what the stock size was. He didn’t know. I told him to check the driver’s door jamb. He did. 225/60R17. I pulled up the Tire Size Calculator on my laptop.

We plugged in the numbers. 225/60R17 has a diameter of about 27.6 inches. 225/55R18 has a diameter of about 27.7 inches. That’s a difference of about 0.4%. Wait – that’s actually within Subaru’s spec. I stared at the screen. That wasn’t adding up. “Chris,” I said, “were all four tires the same size?” “Yeah,” he said. “I think so.” “Were they all the same brand? Same model? Same tread depth?” Pause. “The fronts were more worn than the rears. The guy said they had about six months left on them.” Pete grabbed the phone. “That’s your problem right there. The tires were the same size on paper, but the worn fronts had a smaller circumference than the rears. Just enough to cook the diff.” I ran the comparator with the worn tire diameter estimated. A tire with 6/32 of tread remaining instead of the original 10/32 of new tread. That’s a difference of about 0.3 inches in diameter. Not much. But on a Subaru, any variance over about a quarter inch puts the center differential in the danger zone.

I walked Chris through the comparison. Stock new tires: 27.6 inches. His used fronts: maybe 27.2 inches. Used rears: 27.5 inches because they had more tread. The difference between the smallest and largest was about 0.3 inches. That’s a circumference difference of almost an inch. Every time the wheels turned, the front tires had to spin slightly faster to keep up. The center differential was slipping constantly. For two weeks. At highway speeds. Until it gave up. The repair bill came to $4,100. Pete knocked off a few hundred because he felt bad for the kid. Chris paid with a credit card. He drove home in a loaner car, a beat-up Ford Focus, while his Outback sat in Gary waiting for a rebuilt transmission to arrive from Chicago. I asked Chris if he wanted to sell the used wheels. He said “I already listed them. Someone bought them the same day. A guy with a Forester.” I didn’t have the heart to tell him that guy might be in the same situation in a few months. Chris’s story isn’t rare. Pete says he sees at least one AWD transmission failure per month from mismatched tires. Most are from drivers who bought a used car with mismatched tires already installed, or from owners who replaced two tires instead of four. Some are from people like Chris, who bought used wheels without checking the tread depth variation. “People think tires are just black circles,” Pete told me. “They don’t think about them as precision components. But on an AWD car, they are. The system expects all four to be identical. If they’re not, something breaks.” Subaru’s official spec is that all four tires must be within 2/32 of an inch of tread depth from each other, and the overall diameter must be within a quarter inch. That’s not a suggestion. That’s a requirement. The owners manual says it in plain English. But nobody reads the owners manual. Chris didn’t. He just wanted cool wheels. A few weeks later, Chris got his Outback back. He drove it to work, then to the grocery store, then home. It drove fine. But the transmission was rebuilt, not replaced. Rebuilt means used parts, not new. The warranty was ninety days. He told me he was thinking about selling it. I don’t blame him. Here’s what I want you to remember. If you drive an AWD vehicle – Subaru, Audi, BMW xDrive, Mercedes 4MATIC, Honda CR-V AWD, Toyota RAV4 AWD – never install tires that are more than a quarter-inch different in circumference from stock. And never install two new tires without matching the other two. Always buy four, or find a shop that can shave a new tire to match the tread depth of the others. The Tire Size Calculator and Comparator are free. They take two minutes to use. Chris didn’t use them. He paid $4,100. Don’t be Chris.

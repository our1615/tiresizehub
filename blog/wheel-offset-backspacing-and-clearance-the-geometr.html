Wheel Offset, Backspacing, and Clearance: The Geometry of Proper Fitment

📅 2024-06-12 👤 Ryan Miller ⏱️ 7 min read 🏷️ Concept
The photos came in at 9 PM on a Friday. A guy named Tyler had just installed a new set of wheels on his lifted Silverado. He was thrilled with the look. Then he tried to turn. The tires hit the fender liners with a sound like ripping cardboard. He sent me a video. The truck was parked. He cranked the wheel to the left. The tire rubbed so hard the suspension compressed. He couldn't drive it. “What offset did you buy?” I asked. “I don’t know,” he said. “The guy at the shop said they’d fit.” That sentence, “the guy at the shop said they’d fit,” has cost more money than almost any other phrase in the modification world. The guy at the shop wants to sell wheels. He might know what he’s talking about. He might not. He might be guessing. Tyler learned that the hard way. Wheel offset is the distance from the wheel’s centerline to the mounting surface. Positive offset means the mounting surface is toward the outside of the wheel. Negative offset means it’s toward the inside. Zero offset means it’s exactly at the centerline. Every wheel has an offset stamped on it somewhere – usually on the back of a spoke or on the hub. Look for “ET” followed by a number. ET35 means 35mm positive offset. ET-12 means 12mm negative offset. Backspacing is related. It’s the distance from the back edge of the wheel to the mounting surface. Measure it with a straight edge and a ruler. Lay the wheel face down, put a straight edge across the back lip, measure down to the mounting hub. That’s your backspacing. More backspacing means the wheel sits further inboard. Less backspacing means it pushes out. Tyler’s truck came from the factory with 18x8.5 wheels, +24 offset, about 5.5 inches of backspacing. He bought 20x10 wheels with -12 offset, about 4.5 inches of backspacing. That’s a huge difference. The new wheels stuck out almost two inches further than stock. That’s why they rubbed. The tires were swinging in a wider arc than the fenders could handle. The Wheel Offset Calculator would have shown him this before he spent any money.

I walked him through the numbers. Plug in stock offset: +25. Plug in new offset: -12. The calculator showed that the new wheels would sit 37mm further out from the hub – about an inch and a half. That’s a lot. The outer edge of the tire would be almost two inches further out than stock. The fender liner couldn’t accommodate that much extra width. He had two options. Buy new wheels with a less aggressive offset – something like +12mm – or trim the fender liners and install aftermarket upper control arms to shift the wheel forward. He didn’t want to trim. He returned the wheels and ordered a set with +12mm offset. That put him 13mm further out than stock – about half an inch. The tires still looked aggressive. They didn’t rub. Problem solved. Tyler also changed his tire size from 275/65R18 to 285/55R20. The comparator showed the new tire was about 10mm wider and almost the same diameter. That extra width, combined with the aggressive offset, made the rubbing worse. If he had kept his stock tire width, the -12 offset might have cleared. Maybe. He learned the hard way. He ordered new wheels with a +12 offset. Those stuck out about half an inch further than stock – still aggressive, but not ridiculous. They didn’t rub. He kept his tires. He was happy. He told me “I wish I had used the calculator before buying.” Another common mistake is buying wheels with too much backspacing for a lifted truck. A 4-inch lift often needs less backspacing to keep the tires from hitting the sway bar. The offset calculator shows you the change. I remember a guy named Steve. He lifted his Ram 2500 and bought wheels with stock offset. The tires hit the radius arms at full lock. He used the offset calculator to see how much he needed to move outward. The calculator said 10mm would clear. He bought 10mm wheel spacers. Problem solved. But wheel spacers add another variable. They can cause vibration if not hub-centric. They can put extra stress on wheel studs. Use quality spacers or buy the right offset wheels in the first place. Tyler’s new tires were about the same diameter as stock, so his gearing was fine. But if you go up two inches in tire diameter, you need to check your effective gear ratio. Back to offset. Scrub radius is the term that nobody uses but everyone should know. It’s the distance between the tire’s contact patch centerline and the steering axis pivot point. Change the offset, you change the scrub radius. Change the scrub radius, you change how the steering feels. Too much positive offset – stock – gives you light steering and good stability. Too much negative offset gives you heavy steering, more kickback over bumps, and a wandering feeling on the highway. I drove a lifted truck once that pulled left every time I hit a bump. The owner blamed the alignment. The alignment was fine. The offset was wrong. The wheels had -24mm offset. The scrub radius was huge. Every bump tugged the steering wheel. He didn’t want to change the wheels, so he lived with it. That’s his choice. But he should have known before he bought. The Tire Load Index Calculator is also important when changing wheels.

If you’re buying aftermarket wheels, make sure they’re rated for your vehicle’s weight. Cheap wheels from unknown brands might not have a load rating. Stick with major brands. Tyler’s new wheels had a load rating of 2,500 pounds each. His truck needed 2,200. Fine. So what’s the right way to choose offset? First, find your stock offset and backspacing. Look on the back of your current wheel. It’s stamped there. Or measure it. Second, decide how much outward stance you want. A 10-20mm reduction in offset (going from +25 to +10) will push the wheel out about half an inch. That’s noticeable but unlikely to rub on most trucks. A 30-40mm reduction will push the wheel out over an inch. That’s where rubbing starts. A 50mm reduction – going from +25 to -25 – pushes the wheel out almost two inches. Expect rubbing. Expect trimming. Third, use the offset calculator to simulate the change. It’s free. It’s fast. It’s accurate. Fourth, if you’re also changing tire size, use the tire size comparator to see the width and diameter change. Combine that with the offset change to estimate clearance. Fifth, if you’re not sure, ask someone who has done the exact same setup on the exact same truck. Forums are your friend. Tyler learned this the hard way. He spent $400 on return shipping for the wrong wheels. He could have avoided that with ten minutes of research. Don’t trust the tire shop. Don’t trust the wheel website. Don’t trust the guy at the car meet who says “they’ll fit.” Trust the numbers. Measure. Calculate. Verify.

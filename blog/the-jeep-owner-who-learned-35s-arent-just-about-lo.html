The Jeep Owner Who Learned 35s Aren't Just About Looks

📅 2024-06-10 👤 Ryan Miller ⏱️ 5 min read 🏷️ Story
Jake had a 2018 Jeep Wrangler JLU. Lifted two inches. Aftermarket bumpers. A winch he'd used exactly once, to pull a bush out of his grandmother's yard. He wanted bigger tires. Not because he needed them. Because they looked cool. He posted in a Facebook group: “What size tires can I fit with a 2-inch lift?” The group said 35s. Some said 37s if he trimmed. Jake bought 35s. Cooper Discoverer STT Pro. Mounted them on his stock wheels. Paid a shop $200 for mounting and balancing. He was thrilled. The first week was fine. The tires hummed on the highway. His gas mileage dropped from 19 to 14. He didn't care. They looked aggressive. Then he took a road trip from Detroit to Traverse City. Four hours each way. Halfway there, he noticed the transmission hunting for gears. The engine felt sluggish. At highway speeds, the Jeep struggled to maintain 70 mph on slight inclines. He downshifted manually. The RPMs screamed. He made it to Traverse City, but the Jeep felt wrong. He called me. “Ryan, my Jeep feels gutless. Did I make a mistake with these tires?” I asked him what gears he had. He didn't know. He'd bought the Jeep used. Never checked the axle ratio. I told him to find the build sheet or crawl under and look for the metal tag on the differential. He found it. 3.45 gears. That's the stock ratio for a Sport model with the automatic transmission and standard tires. With 35-inch tires, his effective gear ratio dropped to about 2.90. His engine was lugging. His transmission was slipping. His fuel economy was shot. Jake had two options. Re-gear the axles to 4.56 or 4.88 – about $2,500 parts and labor. Or sell the 35s and go back to 33s. He didn't have $2,500. He listed the 35s on Marketplace the next day. The Tire Size Comparator could have saved him.

He could have compared his stock tire size (say 255/70R18 or 32-inch) to 35s. The comparator would have shown him the difference in revolutions per mile. Stock tires turn about 630 revs per mile. 35s turn about 590. That's a 6% reduction. For every mile, his wheels turn 6% fewer times. That means his engine and transmission have to work harder to push the same distance. It's like towing a trailer that isn't there. If Jake had used the Gear Ratio Calculator before buying, he would have seen that 3.45 gears with 35s put him well outside the ideal RPM range for the 3.6L Pentastar engine. That engine makes peak torque around 4,800 RPM. With 35s, he'd be cruising at 2,000 RPM at 70 mph – too low for the powerband. The engine was lugging. That causes heat, wear, and poor economy.

Jake learned his lesson. He sold the 35s for $800. Bought a set of 33-inch all-terrains. The difference was immediate. His Jeep drove like stock again. Fuel economy came back to 17. Transmission stopped hunting. He told me later, “I thought bigger tires were just about clearance. I didn't know they changed everything – gearing, acceleration, mpg, even braking distance.” That's the part that nobody puts in the Instagram captions. 35s look great. But they also add 30-50 pounds per corner. That unsprung weight affects suspension response. It increases stopping distance. It accelerates wear on ball joints, tie rods, and wheel bearings. It's not just a bolt-on upgrade. It's a system change. If you're considering 35s or 37s, here's what you need to check first. What's your current gear ratio? Look for the tag on the differential. Common ratios: 3.21, 3.45, 3.73, 4.10, 4.56. Multiply your ratio by your tire size change. A formula: New effective ratio = (Old tire diameter / New tire diameter) × Old gear ratio. If the result is below 3.50 on a Wrangler with 35s, you need a re-gear. What's your transmission? The automatic 8-speed can handle 35s with 3.73 gears. The older 5-speed needs 4.10 or higher. Manual transmissions need even deeper gears because they can't downshift automatically. What's your axle type? Dana 30 front and Dana 35 rear on older Jeeps won't survive 37s. You'll snap axle shafts. A Dana 44 is fine for 35s, marginal for 37s. A Dana 60 is for 40s. Jake's Jeep had a Dana 30 front and Dana 44 rear. The Dana 30 would have been fine with 35s, but the gearing killed the driving experience. He didn't need a re-gear for strength. He needed it for drivability. A lot of Jeep owners skip the math because they're excited. They see a picture of a lifted Wrangler on 35s and they want that exact look. They don't realize that Jeep might have 4.88 gears, a re-geared transfer case, aftermarket axles, and a hemi swap. The photo doesn't tell that story. Jake's story has a happy ending. He found a buyer for the 35s. He bought 33s. He's now saving for a proper re-gear to 4.56 so he can run 35s again – the right way. He told me, “Next time, I'm doing the math before I spend the money.” If you're upgrading tires on a truck or SUV, do the math first. Use the comparator. Use the gear ratio calculator. Then decide if the look is worth the trade-off.

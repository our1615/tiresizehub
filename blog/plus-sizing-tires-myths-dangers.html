Plus Sizing Tires Myths Dangers

2026-07-23 · 3 min read · General
General

Your buddy swears his 20-inch rims make his car handle better. My alignment rack says otherwise. I have been working on cars for fifteen years, and I have seen more problems caused by plus-sizing than benefits. It is not that bigger wheels are always bad. It is that most people do it wrong, for the wrong reasons, and then blame the car when things go sideways.

Plus-sizing means putting larger wheels with lower-profile tires on your car. 18s to 19s. 19s to 20s. The rim gets bigger, the sidewall gets thinner. It looks cool. I will give it that. The wheel fills the wheel well. The stance is aggressive. It photographs well for Instagram. But handling? Comfort? Safety? Those often get worse, not better.

Here is why. A thinner sidewall has less cushion. Less absorption. Less ability to flex over bumps. That means more vibration in the cabin. More noise. More impact force transmitted to your suspension components. I have replaced more ball joints and wheel bearings on cars with plus-sized wheels than I can count. The owners never connect the dots. They just think their car is "wearing out."

And handling? On a smooth track, yes, a lower-profile tire can improve response. But on real roads — Michigan roads, with potholes that have their own zip codes — a thin sidewall can not absorb impacts. The tire skips. The contact patch shrinks. You actually lose grip. I put a car on my alignment rack last month that had 20s on a car designed for 18s. The camber was off. The toe was wandering. The owner complained about "vague steering." No kidding. His tires were fighting the road instead of working with it.

Speedometer error is another gotcha. Bigger wheels roll farther per revolution. Your speedometer reads low. You think you are doing 70, you are doing 74. Get pulled over, "but officer, my speedometer said 70" is not a defense. Plus, your odometer under-reads, which affects resale value and warranty coverage.

If you want bigger wheels, do it right. Stay within 3% of the original overall diameter. Match the load rating. Get the alignment checked immediately after. And accept that you are trading comfort for looks. That is fine. Just know the trade.

I also want to address the "staggered fitment" trend — bigger wheels in the back than the front. It looks cool on sports cars. It is a nightmare for daily drivers. Different sized tires front and rear mean you can not rotate them. The rear tires wear out twice as fast as the fronts. And on all-wheel-drive cars, staggered fitment can damage the differential because the front and rear wheels are rotating at different speeds. I had a customer with an Audi who put 20s in the rear and 19s in the front. His differential failed at 40,000 miles. The repair cost $4,200. His "cool" staggered setup cost him more than the wheels themselves.

Load rating is another thing people ignore. Every tire has a load index — a number that tells you how much weight it can carry. Most passenger tires are rated for 1,300-1,500 pounds per tire. That sounds like a lot, but remember: that is per tire, and your car has four. A 4,000-pound car with four passengers and luggage can push the limit. Plus-sized wheels often use lower-profile tires with lower load ratings because the smaller sidewall can not support as much weight. I have seen 20-inch wheels with load ratings 200 pounds lower than the stock 18s. That is 800 pounds less capacity for the whole car. On a family sedan, that is dangerous.

And finally, winter performance. Those low-profile 20s with summer tires? They are useless below 40 degrees. The rubber hardens. The grip disappears. You are driving on hockey pucks. I tell every plus-size customer the same thing: keep your stock wheels for winter. Put snow tires on them. Use the 20s for summer show and the 18s for winter go. It costs more upfront. It saves you from a ditch. And it saves your expensive wheels from salt and potholes. That is the smart play. The cool play is expensive. And sometimes, expensive means a tow truck.

— Mike Donovan

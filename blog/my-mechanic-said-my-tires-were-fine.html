My Mechanic Said My Tires Were Fine

2026-06-24 · 3 min read · General
General

My Mechanic Said My Tires Were Fine. My Speedometer Said I Was Going 72. I Was Actually Going 79.

7 miles per hour. That's how much my speedometer was off. At 70 mph on the dial, I was actually going 77. At 80, I was going 87. And I had been driving like this for 8 months, completely unaware, because my mechanic said my tires were "fine" and I trusted him.

I live in Detroit. I drive a 2019 Ford F-150. Last fall, I decided to upgrade my wheels. I bought 20-inch aftermarket rims with 275/55R20 tires. They looked great. They filled the wheel wells. They made the truck look aggressive and capable. My mechanic mounted them, balanced them, and said I was good to go. He didn't mention the speedometer. He didn't mention the odometer. He didn't mention that changing tire size affects everything.

I found out in the worst possible way. I was driving on I-75 near Toledo, passing through a construction zone. The speed limit was 55. I was going 62 on my speedometer — 7 over, like everyone else. But my actual speed was 69. A state trooper pulled me over. "Do you know how fast you were going?" I said 62. He said 69. I said my speedometer said 62. He said, "Your speedometer is wrong." He was right. He showed me the radar. 69. I got a ticket. And a lesson.

I went home and did the math. My stock tires were 265/60R18. My new tires were 275/55R20. The diameter difference was 1.2 inches. That doesn't sound like much. But it changes the revolutions per mile by 4.3%. Which means my speedometer reads 4.3% low. At 70 mph, that's 3 mph. At 80, that's 3.4 mph. But the error compounds with the odometer. Every 1,000 miles on my odometer, I've actually driven 1,043 miles. My fuel economy calculation is off. My maintenance schedule is off. My warranty mileage is off.

I called my mechanic. I asked why he didn't mention this. He said, "Most people don't care. They just want the look." I was furious. Most people don't care because they don't know. And they don't know because nobody tells them. The tire shop wants to sell tires. The mechanic wants to mount them. The customer wants the look. And nobody is doing the math.

I recalibrated my speedometer. It cost $180 at a Ford dealer. They plugged in a computer, changed the tire size parameter in the PCM, and now my speedometer is accurate. But I shouldn't have had to do that. The mechanic who mounted the tires should have told me. The tire shop that sold them should have told me. The fact that they didn't is not negligence — it's the industry standard. And the industry standard is wrong.

Here's what I learned. When you change tire size, you change everything. Speedometer. Odometer. ABS calibration. Traction control. Fuel economy. Gear ratio effective change. If you go bigger, your truck feels slower because the effective gear ratio is taller. If you go smaller, it feels faster but revs higher on the highway. These are not cosmetic changes. They are mechanical changes. And they have consequences.

If you're upgrading wheels or tires — and in Detroit, everyone is — do the math first. Use a tire size calculator. Check the diameter difference. Check the speedometer error. Check the odometer variance. If the error is more than 2%, recalibrate. If you can't recalibrate, don't do it. Because a 7 mph speedometer error is not a minor inconvenience. It's a $250 ticket. It's a potential accident. It's a warranty issue waiting to happen.

My truck looks great. The 20-inch rims are aggressive. The tires are wide. But now they also accurate. The speedometer is calibrated. The odometer is correct. And I know exactly how fast I'm going, even when the trooper asks.

— Ryan, from a garage in Detroit where the tires are big and the math is bigger
